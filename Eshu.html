How does the mutualistic relationship between coral reefs and Symbiodinium affect coral reef resilience to environmental stressors such as rising ocean temperatures.

Coral-Symbiodinium relationship

The ocean is filled with many invertebrate species, and corals are one of them. Corals are made of tiny soft polyps. These polyp colonies secrete hard calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) skeletons, which form large structures called coral reefs. The Great Barrier Reef is one of the Earth’s largest and well known reef systems. It is located off the coast of Australia. The productivity of these coral reefs depend on a symbiotic relationship with unicellular dinoflagellates of the genus Symbiodinium (zooxanthellae)(Wooldridge, 2010). This is a classic example of trophic mutualism, where both species exchange necessary resources to maintain productivity and ecological function. The coral provides zooxanthellae with a space within the gastrodermal cell that is protected from external predators.The coral also delivers essential substrates like CO2 and ammonia to the Symbiodinium (Wooldridge, 2010). The zooxanthellae in return provide coral with approximately 95% photosynthetically derived organic compounds, also known as photosynthate (Wooldridge, 2010). Hence this direct interaction improves the corals ability to survive as it improves growth, skeletal formation and increases the ability to manage stress like less nutrient availability and rising sea temperatures(Wooldridge, 2010).

Impact of Marine Heat waves on Acropora millepora population

However, the nutrients and energy exchange between coral and Symbiodinium is sensitive to environmental stress. This can be demonstrated by Acropora millepora species on the Great Barrier Reef. Acropora millepora generally grow on shallow water, usually mid to upper reef slope(Cooper et al., 2011). This habitat especially puts the coral at risk of intense solar radiation and rising ocean temperature. Due to global warming there are more frequent marine heat waves, which means the water gets hot for extended periods. Statistical analysis confirms that these marine heat waves are major drivers of biological change, with the most significant stressors being abnormal temperature fluctuations and high summer averages (P<0.05)(Cooper et al., 2011). Together the UV radiation and high water temp explain 51.3% of the regional variation in the Symbiodinium on the Great Barrier Reef (Cooper et al., 2011).

Effect of rising ocean temperatures

This is because when the ocean temperature increases, stress disrupts the structure of the proteins within the Symbiodinium's chloroplast. Consequently, the chloroplasts are no longer capable of photosynthesizing. Instead of processing the sun's energy, it builds up by forming ROS, toxic oxygen molecules(Pernice et al., 2011). This accumulation of ROS leads to the poisoning of both the coral and the Symbiodinium. Consequently, the coral host is forced to activate apoptosis(Pernice et al., 2011). Which is programmed cell death also known as cell suicide, and is executed by caspase enzymes and regulated by Bcl-2 protein(Pernice et al., 2011). Overall, this causes expulsion of the Symbiodinium from the coral host, causing coral bleaching.

Post-Bleaching Physiology and Mortality Risks

The visible whitening of coral tissue is known as coral bleaching, a process where there is expulsion of symbiotic Symbiodinium. Experimental data shows that the heat stress of 29.5 ºC leads Acropora millepora to become visually paler, resulting in 46% loss of chlorophyll compared to coral colonies in stable environments(Nielsen et al., 2020). Without these photosynthetic activities of the symbionts, the corals are deprived of their primary energy influx. This leads to a starvation state of coral, forcing it to use its stored energy, such as lipids and protein to survive(Nielsen et al., 2020). If the thermal stress continues for a long period, the coral will eventually run out of these energy reserves and die. Therefore, the mutualistic relationship serves as the primary mechanism for the reef resilience, its breakdown under high temperature forces the coral into metabolic deficit, which directly affects its ability to withstand future environmental stressors.

Symbiont Diversity

The strength of a coral to increase ocean temperatures strongly depends on the genetic strain of Symbiodinium occupying its tissue. Symbionts with the heat-sensitive strain, Type C3, exhibit a rapid decline in physiological function compared to those with the heat-tolerant strain, Type C15 (Fisher et al., 2012). Since the symbiont Acropora millepora stain is Type C3, it has more risk of coral bleaching. Therefore, the resilience of this reef essentially depends upon the genetic differences of its symbiotic partner.

Conclusion

Overall, the mutualistic relationship is the primary mechanism that controls coral resilience. If the interaction between coral and Symbiodinium breaks, the coral loses so much energy it can die, but the genetic diversity in its symbiotic partner gives it an opportunity to survive. The likelihood of a coral bleaching or recovering deeply relies on whether it harbors a heat-sensitive or heat-tolerant partner. The stability of this microscopic partnership defines the feature of the reef's ability to be resilient in the face of a warming ocean.
